Driveway Liability Checklist

The first buyer of the day is already walking up your driveway, coffee in one hand, and you are still hunting for the cash box. That is the moment a cracked slab or a loose hose turns a good Saturday into a long conversation you did not want to have.

This is a practical walk of the route people will actually take, from the curb to the checkout table and back to their car. It is not legal advice. Rules on liability, fences, and sidewalks vary, so check your city guide and the official page for your town before you advertise.

Walk the shopper path, not the host path

Hosts cut across the lawn, step over the same hose every morning, and never look down. Shoppers do the opposite. They back up to photograph a dresser, drift toward a garment rack, and watch the item in their hands instead of the ground.

At dusk the evening before, walk the sale as if you have never been there. Start at the street, come up the driveway, circle every table, and leave the same way a loaded-down buyer will leave. Carry a roll of bright tape, a cone or two, and a notepad. Mark every place your foot hesitates.

The four zones that cause the most trouble

1. The approach

  • Raised concrete edges, crumbled driveway corners, and lifted pavers get a strip of neon tape or a cone. If a lip is high enough to catch a toe, make it ugly and obvious.
  • Wet leaves, loose gravel, and that one decorative rock you love will take people down. Sweep, rake, or move them.
  • If cars will park on the street, keep the first ten feet of the driveway clear so people can step off the road without walking into a table leg.

2. Power and cords

If you need power to test a lamp, run a fan, or charge a phone, treat every cord as a tripwire. Tape it flat to the concrete with gaffer tape, run it along the house wall, or skip the powered table and test items at a single, supervised station behind the checkout.

Hoses are honorary cords. Coil them the night before. A still-connected sprinkler is a classic Saturday-morning own-goal.

3. Tables, racks, and the squeeze points

Leave a full adult body-width between tables, even if that means one fewer display. People turn with chairs on their shoulders. Kids cut between racks. Your checkout line will back up into the same aisle everyone else is using, so give that aisle extra room.

Put the heaviest furniture on level ground, with the drawers taped or removed so a curious shopper does not pull a dresser over. If a piece rocks, it does not belong on a slope.

4. The house, the pets, and the side yard

Lock the doors you are not using, including the garage entry into the house if the sale is in the garage. Close off the side yard with a baby gate or a clear sign if it leads to a pool, a workshop, or a dog run. For pets, use the Animal Protocol. For things that invite kids to climb or wander, use the Attractive Nuisance Warning.

If someone gets hurt anyway

Stop the sale traffic in that spot, help the person sit or stay still, and ask what they need. Have a charged phone on the checkout table. Write down what happened while it is fresh: time, where they were standing, what was on the ground, who saw it. That note is for you, and it is easier to write at 10:15 a.m. than at 8 p.m. after the adrenaline fades.

Keep a small first-aid kit at the cash table: bandages, antiseptic wipes, ice pack. You will use the wipes on a scraped knee more often than you will use anything dramatic.

One last pass at opening time

Do the walk again ten minutes before you put the first sign at the curb. Daylight shows cracks you missed under the porch light. If rain rolled through overnight, the same slab can be a slip hazard even if you taped the lip. Wipe slick spots, move cardboard off wet concrete, and only then flip the 'open' sign.

When you are ready to think about who is watching the cash and who is watching the door, read the House Security Lockdown. Safe footing and a locked back door are two halves of the same morning.
