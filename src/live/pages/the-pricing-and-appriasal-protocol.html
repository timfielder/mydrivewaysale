Pricing and Appraisal Protocol

Pricing a driveway sale is the art of being close enough that people will pay, without spending Tuesday night researching the aftermarket for every chipped saucer. You are not running an auction house. You are trying to turn a garage full of decisions into a Saturday full of leaving.

Use this protocol to put a defensible number on the items that deserve it, group the rest, and know when to take the offer. For how to show those prices with colors, use the Color Sticker Pricing Strategy. And check your city guide if your town limits what you may sell, such as unused commercial merchandise.

Sort first, price second

Make four piles, physically, before you touch a sticker:

  1. Research: furniture, power tools, bikes, electronics, instruments, jewelry, and anything you suspect has a real used market.
  2. Standard: the middle of every sale, priced from a simple scale once you know your color or dollar rungs.
  3. Lots: books, hangers, cables, baby clothes, glassware that is fine but not special. These sell as groups.
  4. Out: broken, recalled, expired, hazardous, or so personal that you will regret seeing it in a stranger's cart. These do not go on a table.

If you skip the sort, you will spend twenty minutes on a paperback and two minutes on a table saw. That is backwards.

How to research the pile that actually needs it

For each research item, look up the exact model, then look at sold or recently listed used prices, not the original retail, and not a collector's best-case blog post. Take the middle of the recently sold range, then drop a bit because the buyer has to get it off your driveway today, without a return policy.

Plug the thing in. Lift it. Count the chairs in the set. A missing charger, a crack you only see in daylight, or a bike with a rusted chain is not the same item as the one in a clean listing photo. Adjust down, or move it to the standard pile.

Write the price on a card and slip the card into a sheet protector if the item will sit outside. Paper and dew are not friends. Keep a photo of the model tag in your phone in case a shopper wants to confirm what it is.

A simple scale for everything else

Once the research pieces have their numbers, the rest of the sale can ride a short scale, for example $1, $3, $5, $10, and a few higher tags. Clothes in good shape can go on a per-item price or a 'bag for $X' late in the day. Books often move faster in a $1 box than as individually researched paperbacks, unless you truly have a scarce title.

If two items are close, give them the same price. Shoppers can feel a $4 / $5 / $6 scatter as fussiness, and you will feel it as time you do not get back.

When to take the offer

Decide your floor before the first haggle. A useful default: you will listen at 20 percent off a research item late in the day, and you will say yes faster on standard items if the shopper is also taking a lot. If someone lowballs you at 8:15 a.m., smile and say you are holding the price until afternoon. Early birds can pay early-bird prices.

When a shopper asks you to 'hold' a dresser while they go to the ATM, take a small deposit or let it go back on the floor. A hold with no money is a delay with hope attached.

A 15-minute end-of-day reset

In the last hour, cut the standard pile more aggressively than the research pile. The dresser can still find a buyer next weekend if your city allows a second day or a second sale. The box of cables will not, and it should not come back into the house.
