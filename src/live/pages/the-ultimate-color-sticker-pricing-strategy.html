Color Sticker Pricing Strategy

A driveway sale dies at the cash table when the host has to squint at a smudged '$3 or was it $5?' on a piece of tape, then add seven of those in their head while a line forms. Color stickers exist to end that little drama.

This is a working method: pick a small palette, attach prices to colors, teach every helper the same map, and decide in advance how you will markdown. If you want the addition done by a tap, the free StickerCalc Lite tool is built for this exact system. For what a thing should cost in the first place, use the Pricing and Appraisal Protocol.

Why colors beat handwriting

A color is visible from two tables away. A scrawl is not. Colors also survive a little dew, a little dirt, and a helper who cannot read your nines. Shoppers learn the map once, then they can total a handful of items before they reach you, which makes the line kinder.

Keep the palette small. Four to six colors is plenty. Twelve colors is a mural, and nobody will remember it by 10 a.m.

A sample palette you can steal

Here is a simple map that works for a mixed household sale. Adjust the dollar amounts to your inventory, and write this on a poster at the checkout and at the curb-facing end of the first table.

  • Yellow: $1. Books, mugs, small toys, cables, odd kitchen tools.
  • Green: $3. Nicely working small gadgets, board games with all the pieces, decent hand tools.
  • Blue: $5. Lamps, slow cookers, chairs that are not antiques, a clean basket of kids' clothes.
  • Orange: $10. Bikes in okay shape, power tools that run, a solid side table.
  • Red: $20. The 'come look at this' pieces: dressers, mowers, TVs you have actually plugged in.
  • White: make-me-an-offer, or a custom price written on the sticker if an item does not fit the palette.

If you run a multi-family sale, you can flip this: color means household, and the dollar amount is written on the sticker. That is a good system too, and it is described in Multi-Family Sales. Do not mix the two systems on the same driveway. Pick one map and print it.

How to put the stickers on so they still mean something at 2 p.m.

Stick on a clean, flat spot, not on a price tag you already regret. For fabric, use a hang tag or a card, because a dot on a knit sweater is a fight. For furniture, put a sticker where a shopper can see it without kneeling, and a second one in a drawer so they do not 'lose' the price while they 'think about it.'

Use removable dots if you care about the finish. Use a marker that can handle a little moisture if your weekend might see dew. Pre-bag tiny items in lots of one color so you are not applying 80 individual $1 dots at midnight.

Train the cashier in five minutes

Before you open, have the cashier total a pretend purchase: two yellow, one green, one orange. They should say $17 without looking at you. If they cannot, the poster is too small or the palette is too cute. Then point to the StickerCalc Lite color buttons, if you are using it, and do the same sale as taps. The number should match.

A markdown schedule that does not wreck the system

Decide the markdown before you open, and keep it inside the palette. Example: at 1 p.m., orange becomes blue, blue becomes green, and yellow stays yellow or goes to a $0.50 'fill a bag' rule if you want volume. You are swapping colors, not scribbling new prices on 200 items.

Announce the change on the poster and say it out loud to the line. If you use StickerCalc, update the color values when you markdown, or you will collect yesterday's prices with today's stickers.
