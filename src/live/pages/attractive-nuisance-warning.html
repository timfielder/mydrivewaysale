Attractive Nuisance Warning

A trampoline in the side yard does not care that your sale is about old Pyrex. Neither does the riding mower you parked 'just for a minute' with the key in it. Kids follow interesting objects, and a driveway sale is an all-you-can-see buffet of them.

You do not need a law textbook to run a safer sale. You need a habit of spotting the objects that pull people in, then staging them so curiosity does not turn into a climb, a swallow, or a ride. Local rules still matter, especially around pools, fences, and sidewalks, so check your city guide too.

Make a 'magnets' list the night before

Walk your displays with one question: would a seven-year-old stop, touch, or climb this without asking? Write every yes down. Typical magnets:

  • Anything with a seat or handlebars: bikes, scooters, riding mowers, office chairs that spin, folding trampolines.
  • Ladders, step stools, and stacked boxes that look like stairs.
  • Tools that cut, burn, or puncture: saws, box cutters, soldering irons, even a pretty set of kitchen knives on a tablecloth.
  • Chemicals and medicines: paint, bleach, oils, expired vitamins, vape gear, detergent pods.
  • Small parts: beads, magnets, button batteries, loose change in a bowl, dog treats that look like food.
  • Water and drop-offs: a pool gate that does not latch, a decorative pond, an open basement stair.

If it is on the list, it gets a plan: move it, lock it, bag it, or keep it behind the checkout and sell it by request.

Stage the sale so temptation has a fence

Put climbable furniture and bikes on the house side of the driveway, not at the curb. A bike on the apron is an invitation to test-ride into the street. Keep test-rides in a marked patch of driveway, with a grown-up standing there.

Knives, tools, and chemicals go in closed bins behind the cashier, not in a 'free to browse' crate at kid height. If a shopper wants to inspect a saw, you hand it over and you take it back.

Button batteries and magnet sets go in a lidded container, not a candy bowl. The same goes for jewelry with small parts. A clear plastic bin you open on request is slower, and it is worth it.

Pools, workshops, and the 'just this once' side gate

If you have a pool, spa, or steep back-yard drop, the sale stops at a closed, latched gate. A handwritten sign is not a latch. Ask a family member to be the person who notices when a gate is propped open 'for just a second' during a furniture carry.

Workshops and garages full of tools are the same idea. If the sale is in the driveway, the workshop door stays closed. If the sale is in the garage, sharp tools that are not for sale go up on a high shelf or into a locked cabinet before the first shopper arrives.

Talk to the neighbors, then tear it down

Tell the neighbors on either side what time you expect a crowd. If their yard has a trampoline or a dog that bolts, they deserve a warning. Offer to keep testers off their grass. That five-minute chat prevents a lot of yelling at 11 a.m.

At closing, the magnets disappear first. Bikes go inside or get locked. Tool bins go in the house. The ladder you used to hang a banner comes down before you count the cash. A sale is still a sale after the signs come in, at least to a kid walking home from the park.
